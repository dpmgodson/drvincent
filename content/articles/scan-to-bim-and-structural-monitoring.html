Building information modelling is normally discussed as something you do before construction. For most of the building stock that already exists, there is no model at all — and often no reliable drawing either.

Drawings record intent, not outcome

Where drawings survive, they describe what was designed. Buildings depart from their drawings during construction and continue departing afterwards, through alteration, extension and repair. For assessment or retrofit, the difference is exactly the thing you need to know.

Laser scanning and photogrammetry capture what is actually standing. The difficulty is that a point cloud is not a model: it is several hundred million coordinates with no notion of what is a column and what is a pipe.

What the mapping problem looks like

Turning captured geometry into structured model elements is where the real work sits, and our review examines the techniques and mapping strategies available, organised by where they serve the building life cycle. Some stages tolerate approximation — a facilities model does not need structural fidelity. Structural assessment does, and the acceptable error is much smaller.

Why it belongs next to monitoring

A geometric model of the building as it stands is a baseline. Monitoring is what happens to that baseline over time. Kept separately, one is a snapshot and the other is a stream of readings with no spatial context.

Joined, a crack identified in a photograph has a location on a specific member, and a change measured this year can be compared against the same member's geometry last year. That is the direction this work points: models that carry condition, not only shape.
