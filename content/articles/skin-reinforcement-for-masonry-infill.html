Walk through almost any Indian town and the standard building is the same: a reinforced concrete frame with masonry infill. The frame is engineered. The wall between the columns usually is not — it is treated as a partition, a thing that divides space rather than carries anything.

The wall does not know it is non-structural

Once built, the infill stiffens the frame considerably. Under lateral loading it participates whether or not the designer intended it to, acting roughly as a diagonal strut between the corners of the panel. Because it was never detailed for that, it can crush at the corners, shear through its bed joints, or fail out of plane and fall — which is the failure mode that injures people, and it can happen while the frame itself is still standing.

Demolishing and rebuilding is not a serious answer for a building stock this large. The question is what can be done to the wall that already exists.

Skin reinforcement

My doctoral research examined confining the infill using cost-effective skin reinforcement: wire mesh — the ordinary kind — applied to the wall face, embedded in mortar and anchored into the surrounding frame. The mesh gives the panel tensile capacity across its face and holds it together once cracking begins.

Panels were tested under cyclic loading and compared against unreinforced and confined alternatives. The in-plane resistance improved, and the panel held together after cracking rather than shedding material. The added mass was small, which matters, because anything heavy added to a building to improve its seismic performance partly defeats itself.

Making it usable by designers

Test results on their own do not change practice. The work was therefore generalised into an equivalent-strut width for wire-meshed masonry infill under dynamic loading — a form a designer can put into an analysis without replicating the experiment — and checked numerically.

Why cheapness is a technical requirement

A strengthening method that needs proprietary materials, specialist contractors or equipment unavailable in the district will not be applied to the buildings that need it most. Chicken mesh and mortar are available in every town in Tamil Nadu, and a mason can apply them. That constraint shaped the research from the start, and it is the same constraint that later made reinforcement integration in printed concrete an interesting problem rather than an academic one.
