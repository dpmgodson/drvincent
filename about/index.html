Who I am

I am a civil engineering academic, and that has turned out to be several jobs.

I teach structural engineering at Karunya Institute of Technology and Sciences. I also coordinate the Internal Quality Assurance Cell, lead Institution Relations, supervise doctoral research, review for several journals, and teach in church. These are not five careers. They are one disposition applied in five places.

The disposition is an engineer's: look at what is actually there, work out what carries the load, and strengthen that. It applies as well to a department as to a wall.

My journey

Masonry taught me most of what I know about everything else.

I finished my B.E. in 2006 and my M.Tech. in structural engineering in 2008, and started teaching the same year. I did not begin with a research plan. I began with a subject I had to teach well enough to be useful, and a set of buildings around Coimbatore that were not behaving the way the textbooks described.

In framed construction across much of South India, the concrete frame is designed and the masonry infill is not. The wall is treated as a partition and then behaves structurally — stiffening the frame, attracting force it was never detailed to carry, and failing in ways that endanger people even when the frame survives. My doctoral research, completed in 2018, asked whether that wall could be restrained using materials a mason already has on the site: wire mesh, mortar, and careful anchorage into the frame.

The answer was yes, and the work that established it — cyclic testing, an equivalent-strut formulation, numerical validation — is the foundation everything since has been built on. It led to a granted patent on reinforcement at the beam–column joint. It led, less obviously, to the printing work: once you have spent years asking how to get reinforcement into a wall economically, a technology that deposits concrete without reinforcement is not an achievement but a problem statement.

Somewhere in those years the centre of gravity shifted. I noticed that the students I had taught were doing more in the world than my papers were, and that the institution I worked in could be strengthened with the same attention I had been giving to wall panels. I took on quality assurance. I took on admissions. I took on Institution Relations. None of it was a departure from engineering.

What I believe

Knowledge that stays in a journal has not finished its work.

I believe research should be answerable to the place it comes from. The strengthening methods I have worked on are deliberately cheap, because a method that requires equipment nobody in the district owns has not solved the problem it claims to solve.

I believe teaching the fundamentals is not conservatism. Additive manufacturing, machine learning and mixed reality all rest on statics, materials and mechanics. A student who has those can learn any of the rest; a student who has only the rest can evaluate none of it.

I believe institutions are built the same way structures are — slowly, in detail, by people who will not be the ones cutting the ribbon.

And I believe that service is not what you do after the professional work is done. It is what the professional work is for.

Vision and mission

What I am trying to do.

Vision. Construction in India that is safer, more affordable and more intelligent — and students who are equipped to make it so.

Mission. To teach the fundamentals rigorously, to research problems that matter where I live, to build institutions that outlast my part in them, and to use whatever I know in the service of people.

He builds knowledge. He builds structures. He builds institutions. He builds people.
